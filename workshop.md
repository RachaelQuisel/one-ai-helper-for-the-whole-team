## 1. The promise

In the next hour, you'll design the one AI teammate your business actually needs: a shared team bot with a job title, its own seat in your chat app, and a rule that nothing it drafts goes out without your tap. You'll leave with a filled-in Team Bot Card naming its role, what it knows, what it may touch, what needs your approval, and what you'll watch for two weeks.

## 3. Facilitator script

### 0–8 · The week the agent got a desk + the three ideas

Say it in your own words (keep it under eight minutes):

- "This week, the AI agent stopped being a tool you open and became a teammate with a seat. xAI's Grok Team Bots give a bot its own handle in your Slack channel. OpenAI's ChatGPT Space puts your team, the AI, and your always-on agents in one shared workspace. A two-hour course this week built an entire one-person business on these bots: an AI operations lead, an inbox assistant, a sales team. The agent has a desk now."
- "At the same time, the price arrived. OpenAI halved the included usage on its two-hundred-dollar plan and added a five-hundred-dollar tier, because every agent action now has a meter on it. And somebody claimed their ChatGPT Dot emailed city officials about a lease question. Unconfirmed, but instructive: the week the agent got a desk is also the week we learned the desk needs rules."
- "Yesterday we learned the AI step: one interpretive job inside a workflow, with a checkbox leash. Today the agent gets a name, a role, and a seat in the channel where your team already talks. But the leash is the same one. The bot proposes. A human disposes."

Three big ideas in plain words. Say them, then point at them all hour:

1. **The bot is a role, not a task.** Don't ask it to "do some research." Give it a job title and a job description: the inbox assistant reads overnight email and drafts replies. The morning briefer reads the calendar and drops five bullets at eight. A role you can evaluate. A task you can't.
2. **Shared brain, private chats.** Everyone on the team talks to the same bot, and it learns from the shared context: your files, your instructions, your way of writing. But each person's conversations stay private. The bot knows the business. It doesn't gossip.
3. **Drafts first, always.** Nothing the bot writes goes to a customer, a vendor, or the public without a human tap. The bot drafts. You decide. That rule never expires, no matter how good the drafts get.

"Molly Graham, the management thinker on Lenny's podcast this week, gave us the frame: the human sandwich. Vision at the top, AI in the middle, humans at the end. Your team bot lives in the middle. You stay at both ends."

### 8–18 · The anatomy of a team bot

"Every team bot has the same five parts. Learn the parts and you can read any agent anyone tries to sell you."

Draw this on the board or a slide, left to right:

**1. The role.** A job title and a one-paragraph job description. (Example: "Inbox Assistant. Reads overnight email, drafts replies in my voice, queues them for my approval. Never sends.") If you can't write the job description, you're not ready to hire the bot.

**2. The brain.** What it knows: your files, your instructions, your examples. (Example: the price list, the return policy, three emails you wrote that it should sound like.) The brain is shared across the team. Everyone's bot knows the same business.

**3. The hands.** The tools it may use: read the inbox, search the docs, look up an order. Hands are read-only by default. Anything that sends, publishes, spends, or deletes is not a hand. It's a request.

**4. The leash.** Two parts. First: what needs your tap (send, publish, spend, promise, delete). Second: who sees what. The bot's conversations with each person stay private, even though the brain is shared. Write both down.

**5. The seat and the meter.** Where it lives: its own handle in the Slack channel (or Teams, or wherever your team already talks). People talk to it like a colleague. And the meter: every agent action costs something, so you watch the usage the way you'd watch a new hire's expense reports. The price is the policy now.

"Notice the shape. Role, brain, hands, leash, seat. The bot is a hire, not a download. You'd never onboard an employee without a job description and spending limits. Don't onboard a bot without them either."

*Facilitator note:* if anyone asks "which product should I use," say: "Grok Team Bots, ChatGPT Dots in a Space, and Hyperagent all converged on the same shape this week: a bot with a role, a chat handle, shared context, and per-user privacy. Pick the one that lives where your team already talks. The card you're building today works for all of them." Keep it to one minute.

### 18–28 · Watch it work

Walk through the two-hour "one-person business on Grok Bots" course, compressed to ten minutes. No installation for attendees: follow-along demo. If you have a Grok or ChatGPT account with bot access, build live; otherwise narrate the course's build with screenshots.

Narrate only the moving parts, in plain words:

1. "Here's the hire." *(Show the bot's role card: "Inbox Assistant. Reads overnight email, drafts replies, queues for approval. Never sends.")* "A job title and a job description. That's the whole interview."
2. "Here's the brain." *(Show the context: three sample emails, the return policy, the price list.)* "It knows how we sound and what we sell. Shared brain, so the whole team gets the same quality."
3. "Here's the seat." *(Show the bot's own handle in a Slack channel.)* "It lives where the team lives. You talk to it like a colleague. Your conversation with it is private."
4. "Here's the leash in action." *(Run it on one real email. Show the draft it queued.)* "The draft is sitting here waiting. Nothing reached a customer. Would you press send on that, edit it, or throw it away?"

Then ask the room: "What would you change about the job description?" That question teaches the whole workshop: the job description is the product, and you're the hiring manager.

*Facilitator note:* If it glitches, narrate the fix: "this is what managing one looks like." Ten minutes max, then open the floor.

### 28–33 · Your questions

Open floor. Answer in plain English. The three questions you'll get, and the answers:

- "What if the bot does something wrong?": It can only do what its role, brain, and hands allow, and the leash says the consequential tools need your tap. The worst it can do unsupervised is write a bad draft that sits in a queue. That's the whole point of drafts-first.
- "What does it cost?": Every agent action is metered now. The read-only work is cheap; the doing is where the money goes. That's actually the guardrail: nobody runs an unsupervised bot on a metered plan. Watch the usage page for two weeks like you'd watch a new hire's expenses.
- "Should I just wait until this is more mature?": No. The shape has converged: role, brain, hands, leash, seat. That shape will still be the shape in a year. Learn the shape now on one low-stakes role, and you'll be fluent when the products get better.

### 33–58 · Build your own

Everyone works solo or in pairs on the Team Bot Card (see `handout.md`). Circulate with only these prompts:

- "What role would you hire for first? Inbox, morning brief, meeting notes, lead follow-up?"
- "Write the job description in one paragraph. What does it do, and what does it never do?"
- "What does it need to know? Name three files or examples."
- "What needs your tap? Be specific. Sending? Publishing? Spending?"

At minute 53, pull the room together. Three-minute share-out: volunteers read their card in thirty seconds. "My bot is ___. Its job is ___. Needs my tap: ___."

Then Q&A to minute 58, and seed the "what could go wrong" story for anyone who asks:

**What could go wrong.** "This week somebody claimed their ChatGPT Dot emailed city officials about a lease question. Unconfirmed, but the plausibility is the lesson: a bot with hands and no leash is a liability. Last week the FTC opened its first investigation into agents acting without supervision. And Google is shutting down its Opal agent builder on November seventeenth, which is the other lesson: if your bot only exists inside one company's magic box, you don't own it, you're renting it. Your version of the supervisor is much simpler: the job description, the leash, drafts-first for a month, and your own eyes on the usage page. That's the whole safety platform, and it fits on one card."

### 58–60 · Wrap-up and homework

"The bot is a role, not a task. Shared brain, private chats. Drafts first, always."

Homework: fill in the Team Bot Card for the one role you'd hire first. If your product supports it, create the bot and run it drafts-first for two weeks: it reads and drafts, you press every button. Bring back two things: how many drafts you'd actually send, and what the usage cost. If the number is high, you have a coworker. If it's low, you have a very expensive autocomplete, and you learned that for the price of a trial.

*Facilitator note:* hold up a filled Team Bot Card as you say this. "Bring this card back filled in, or better, bring the bot it describes."

## 4. Plain-English word list

Keep this on your facilitator copy. If you're tempted to say the left column, say the right column instead, and only use the product's own word after the plain version.

| Instead of... | Say... |
|---|---|
| Team bot / agent teammate | The hire: a bot with a job title |
| Context / shared context | The brain: what it knows |
| Plugins / tool access | The hands: the tools it may use |
| Approval gate / permissions | The leash: what needs your tap |
| Workspace / Space | The seat: where it lives in your chat app |
| Metered usage / credits | The meter: what every action costs |
| Per-user privacy | Private chats: your conversation stays yours |
| Onboarding | The interview: role, brain, hands, leash, seat |
| Multi-agent / agent crew | A team of hires, each with one role |
| Observability / audit log | The receipts: what it read, what it drafted, what you approved |
